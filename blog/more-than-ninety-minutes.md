**Final score: England 2, DR Congo 1**

Somewhere in Lagos tonight, a boy in an England shirt is screaming himself hoarse for Harry Kane. A few streets away, a woman who has never set foot in Kinshasa is watching Yoane Wissa the way people watch a miracle, because DR Congo, in its first ever World Cup knockout appearance after fifty two years away, is doing something no one prepared them for: making England sweat.

By the time the final whistle blows, the scoreline will be the least interesting thing that happened.

A Nigerian boy who roots for England against DR Congo, no matter what, didn't make a small aesthetic choice about which shirt he likes better. He is rehearsing, in miniature, a much older decision: which side of history he has been trained to stand on, this training is in small effect of being brought up watching the Premier League, and knowing the names of all English Player, and barely knowing any of DR Congo's players except possibly Yoane Wissa and maybe Axel Tuanzebe. And if that training runs deep enough, unexamined enough, it will not stop at football. It will show up in which passport he chases, which accent he softens, which version of himself he presents in a room full of people who do not look like him. Root against DR Congo tonight, and you may be practicing, without knowing it, for a lifetime of rooting against your own reflection.

My claim may sound dramatic until you look at where it comes from.

England's football team is not an accident of geography, some of it has roots in the same colonial ledger that left the Congo, and Nigeria, and most of the continent, playing catch up on infrastructure our own resources helped build elsewhere.

So when a Nigerian cheers for England purely because it is the "bigger" team, the more "serious" footballing nation, he is often, without meaning to, accepting a hierarchy that was assigned to his continent from the outside. He is treating the outcome of that history as if it were simply the natural order of talent and merit, rather than the result of who got to extract from whom.

## Black Skin, White Masks

Frantz Fanon wrote about this seventy years ago, long before satellite television made it possible to watch the psychological pattern play out live on a screen. His central insight in his book *Black Skin, White Masks* was that colonialism did not only take land and labor. It installed a value system in the mind of the colonized, one where whiteness, and the institutions built by white Europe, become the unconscious benchmark for what counts as competent, cultured, or worth aspiring to. The colonized subject, in Fanon's account, does not simply resent the colonizer. Part of him wants to become the colonizer, be associated with him, or at least to be recognized by him, because recognition itself has been redefined as something only the colonizer can grant.

That is the deeper psychological implication of a boy defaulting to England. It is not really about Kane's finishing or Bellingham's vision. It is about where he has been taught to locate legitimacy.

## Your Excellence: Genius or Genetics?

It would be easy to dismiss this as something that lives entirely inside the psychology of the colonized, a wound that only Africans need to heal. But the pattern shows up on the other side too, in how the game itself is narrated.

In 2020, the sports research firm RunRepeat, working with the Professional Footballers' Association, analyzed over two thousand commentary statements across eighty matches in Europe's top four leagues. The pattern was stark and consistent. Commentators were far more likely to praise lighter skinned players for intelligence, work ethic, and quality. Darker skinned players were roughly six and a half times more likely to be described in terms of power, and over three times more likely to be described in terms of speed. Skill got quietly reassigned to one group. Physicality got quietly reassigned to the other.

That is not an accident of individual commentators being careless. It is the same old hierarchy, doing what it has always done: deciding, before a ball is even kicked, whose excellence gets called genius and whose excellence gets called genetics.

## Beyond the Pitch

None of this stays contained to ninety minutes. The same reflex that reads a Black player's brilliance as "raw talent" rather than "vision" is the reflex that reads an African university degree as somehow less rigorous than a European one, that treats a Nigerian accent as something to be neutralized before a client call, that makes a visa officer scrutinize an African applicant's intentions more closely than a European one's, even when the paperwork is identical. It is the same hierarchy showing up in a hiring panel, a scholarship committee, a landlord's hesitation. The football pitch is just the most public stage on which the pattern gets performed, and therefore the easiest place to see it clearly.

Picture the interview room first. Two candidates, same qualifications, same years of experience, one submitted a CV under a name that reads as Chukwuemeka, the other under a name that reads as James. Bertrand and Mullainathan's landmark field experiment, sending nearly five thousand fictitious resumes to real job ads in Boston and Chicago, found that white sounding names received fifty percent more callbacks than Black sounding ones, a gap the researchers calculated was worth as much as eight additional years of experience. Nobody in that hiring pipeline believed themselves biased. The resume simply carried a different unconscious weight before a single line of it was read.

Picture the visa window next. A Nigerian passport currently opens roughly forty four countries visa free. A British one opens close to a hundred and eighty three. That is not a measure of how trustworthy either traveler is, it is a line item decided by treaties negotiated between governments long before either of them was born. So the Nigerian professional heading to a conference gathers bank statements, an invitation letter, proof of a return ticket, proof of property, proof he intends to come back, then waits weeks for an embassy interview slot. His British counterpart fills out a form the night before and boards the plane.

That alone already subconsciously piles unto the mind of the Nigerian professional, "I am less, I need to apply to do this, that etc, I need to please the white to get ahead, or go to these places, I need to act a certain way to be accepted," it piles into your conscious or subconscious inferiority complex.

Also picture this: a Nigerian analyst makes a sharp point on a strategy call, and the first reply is "wow, your English is really good," meant as a compliment. He was raised speaking it as a first language, alongside two others. Ten minutes later a colleague with a similar point but a Home Counties accent gets "great insight," nothing else attached. One comment complimented the packaging. The other simply received the content.

Lastly, picture this: for a performance review in a global workplace, the African team member gets rated diligent, reliable, a hard worker. The European colleague with comparable output gets rated strategic, a natural leader, someone with vision. Power and pace again, dressed for the office instead of the pitch. Neither review is dishonest exactly, but each is quietly building a different case file for who gets developed toward leadership and who gets kept exactly where they are, useful and unpromoted.

None of these rooms know about each other. That is what makes the pattern durable, no single decision maker experiences it as a pattern, only as one isolated, reasonable feeling call. Line the interview, the visa window, the meeting room, and the performance review up next to each other, though, and a shape appears that no single instance could show on its own.

## Direct and Indirect Impact of Foreign Cultural Influence

It would be dishonest to just say every Nigerian in an England shirt is a case study in colonized psychology, or as if every setback the continent faces is purely a matter of what is inside people's heads. Some of it is simpler than that. A generation raised on the Premier League, broadcast into every living room and viewing center from Lagos to Lubumbashi, will know Bellingham's touch before it knows most Congolese players' names, and that is a fact about exposure and marketing reach, not proof of self hatred. And the gap between Europe and Africa is not only psychological. Decades of uneven infrastructure investment, extracted resources, and unequal trade terms would produce real disparities in credentials, funding, and opportunity even if every bias in every human mind vanished overnight. This is not just psychological, there is an economic explanation too, and they both feed each other.

## The Long Game

An African rooting for an African team, consistently, tournament after tournament, is not just an emotional preference. It is a way of deliberately building a record, because belief does not update from a single data point, it accumulates from many.

Psychologist Albert Bandura called part of this mechanism vicarious self-efficacy: people do not only draw confidence from their own achievements, they draw it from watching people they identify with succeed. Every time a team from the continent goes further than expected, it becomes evidence against an old internal prior, a quiet, inherited sense that says we do not get this far. One result does not overwrite that. A pattern of results starts to.

There is a simple, striking experiment that shows this in action. Researchers gave Black and white Americans the same hard verbal test at several points during Obama's 2008 campaign, right after his nomination speech, and again after he won. Black participants' scores jumped closer to white participants', on some occasions almost closing the gap completely. Nothing about the test changed, only what people had just watched someone who looked like them achieve on the biggest stage there is. It works like weight lifting for a few days, the quiet voice saying "people like me don't get this far" getting harder to believe, freeing up focus that had been spent managing doubt instead of solving the problem in front of them. One sufficiently visible counterexample was enough to temporarily loosen a pattern that had held for years.

Social identity theory, from Henri Tajfel's research, supplies the other half. People draw real self-esteem from the standing of the groups they belong to, not only from personal achievement. A continent's collective sense of itself is genuinely, measurably tied to how its representatives are seen performing on shared stages. That is why these tournaments carry weight far beyond the players and the ninety minutes, they are doing identity work for millions of people who will never kick a ball at that level.

## Korea's Example

There is precedent for how long this actually takes. South Korea spent decades being read through a narrow, often dismissive Western lens. It was not one film, one company, or one artist that changed that. It was Samsung's engineering reputation, then Korean cinema, then K-pop, then a single streaming show that became a global phenomenon, stacking one achievement on another until the general perception of the country itself had to update. That took the better part of two generations, not one news cycle.

Losses tend to get absorbed as confirmation of the old story, wins as exceptions rather than proof the model is wrong. The accumulation strategy is real, but it likely needs to cross a threshold of repetition before it rewrites the underlying story rather than just the record book. One historic run does not do it. A decade of African teams consistently reaching quarterfinals and finals starts to.

But Africans need to confirm this in the way they interpret results of global competitions. DR Congo can say "we did better this time" instead of "we failed woefully," and Africans can say "DR Congo could have won England today," which makes it psychologically possible to win them tomorrow.

## The Compounding Advantage

There is a reason building on top of a prior achievement works, and it is not sentimental. It is closer to how interest compounds. Sociologist Robert Merton named the pattern in 1968 and called it the Matthew effect, after the parable line about those who have been given more. Studying scientists, he found that researchers who already carried a strong reputation got disproportionately more credit, funding, and future opportunity for work of comparable quality than lesser-known researchers doing the same caliber of work. The advantage was not really about the work. It was about having enough prior standing that new effort had something to attach to. A later review by sociologists Thomas DiPrete and Greg Eirich traced the same mechanism across careers, wealth, and education more broadly, and the finding held: small early differences, given time, become large ones, not because ability diverges but because opportunity keeps responding to whatever record already exists.

This is the part of the argument that makes "just work hard" an incomplete answer. Effort still matters enormously, but effort attached to an existing foundation converts into results faster than the same effort spent from a standing start.

The same mechanics run underneath a single career. Someone with one visible credential, a scholarship, a role at a globally recognized company, a degree from an institution the whole world already trusts, finds the next door slightly easier to open, not because they grew more capable overnight but because that first credential functions as collateral the next opportunity can lend against. Someone without it has to spend a disproportionate share of their effort simply establishing the baseline of trust the first person's résumé line already supplies for free. Two people can work identically hard and still end up in different places, because one person's hours are compounding on an existing base and the other person's hours are mostly still laying the foundation.

That is precisely why DR Congo's result tonight matters beyond the scoreline, and why the earlier argument about building a record was never just about morale. A first World Cup knockout appearance is not only a memory, it is collateral. It is the credential the next Congolese generation gets to build on rather than start beneath. A program with no history at all has to manufacture belief, infrastructure, and results all at once, from zero, which is a genuinely harder problem than adding one more good result to an already respected one.

Cumulative advantage describes probability and cost, not certainty. A single generational talent, a well-timed investment in a domestic league, or one administrator who does the unglamorous structural work can still create a first foothold from a low base, and history has examples of exactly that happening. It simply costs more to create the first data point than to extend an existing one, which is the entire reason the deposits described earlier are worth making deliberately rather than waiting for them to happen on their own.

## What Are You Choosing?

Whatever happens on the scoreboard in Atlanta tonight, DR Congo already did something worth more than three points. They showed up, fifty two years late by no fault of their own, and made the favorite work for every inch. That is the version of the story worth carrying home.

By the final whistle, England had scraped through 2-1, needing a Harry Kane brace in the last quarter of the match to survive a team they were never supposed to be troubled by. DR Congo, playing their first ever World Cup knockout match, had taken the lead inside seven minutes and made one of the tournament favorites work for over an hour before the gap in squad depth finally told. On the scoreboard, that is a loss. In the ledger this piece has been arguing for, it is an entry. It joins the first goal, the first knockout appearance in fifty two years, and a goalkeeper who nearly stole the night, all of it evidence for the next generation's internal prior, whether or not tonight's scoreline is what gets remembered.

This was never only about football. It was also, quietly, about which record we choose to keep building, one match at a time.

## What Do We Do?

Naming the pattern is not the same as changing it. A few things are actually within reach.

1. **Watch differently, and let it be visible.** Broadcasters and sponsors respond to attention, not sentiment. Streaming an African team's knockout run, buying the shirt, filling the group chat with commentary on Wissa instead of only Kane, all of it becomes a data point that rights holders and advertisers can measure. Vicarious self-efficacy needs an audience to work on. Give it one deliberately, not only when the underdog story is already trending.

2. **Curate what the next generation sees before someone else curates it for them.** A child who has watched African engineers, footballers, founders, and scientists as a normal, unremarkable part of the background will not need convincing later that excellence looks like that too. This is not about hiding England highlights from him. It is about making sure Wissa's name is as familiar in that house as Kane's on a random Tuesday, not only during a World Cup run.

3. **Say the specific thing out loud when the pattern shows up.** Not "that's racist," which tends to end conversations, but the specific comparison: you called her strategic and him diligent for the same result, walk me through that. Research on bias tends to agree that naming the exact mechanism, rather than the general accusation, is what actually gets a decision revisited.

4. **Push for blind process wherever you have any leverage over it.** Blind resume screening, structured interview scoring, named criteria for promotion decided before anyone's face is in the room. These are unglamorous fixes, but they are the ones with real evidence behind them, and if you sit on a hiring panel or run a small team, you likely have more power to install one than you think.

5. **Build the record on purpose, not by accident.** Support African leagues, founders, researchers, and case studies deliberately and consistently, the way you would fund a savings account rather than wait for windfalls. One win does not move the prior. A decade of visible, repeated ones does, and the section on the long game earlier in this piece was not a metaphor, it was a mechanism. Somebody has to keep making the deposits.

None of this closes the passport gap overnight, forty four countries against a hundred and eighty three, visa free. But nobody needs that whole gap fixed before they're allowed to change what they expect to see. What actually moves it is choosing, on purpose, which wins get noticed and repeated, again and again, until what used to feel like the exception starts feeling like the pattern.
